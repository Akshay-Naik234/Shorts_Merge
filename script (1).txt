[soft] You can walk into the AMAZON and within minutes lose your sense of direction. The trees close in around you. The ground is wet. The air is heavy. You hear insects everywhere, birds somewhere above the canopy, and sometimes a rustle in the vegetation that you can't explain. You keep moving, but eventually you realize something uncomfortable. You don't really know where you are anymore. [pause] For centuries, outsiders entered this forest and tried to understand what they were looking at. Some came searching for gold. Others came looking for rivers, cities, or a way through. And most of them saw an enormous wall of green. But there was something they couldn't see. [breathe] Beneath that canopy were traces of roads, settlements, canals, and monumental structures built by people who lived here centuries ago. The strange thing is that the forest didn't make those things disappear. It simply covered them. And once scientists found a way to look beneath the trees, the Amazon started telling a very different story. One of the technologies that changed that was LiDAR. Instead of relying on ordinary photographs, LiDAR sends laser pulses toward the ground and uses the returning signals to map the landscape. Even when thick vegetation blocks the view from above, some of those laser pulses can reach the ground. Researchers can then create a digital model of the terrain and strip away much of the vegetation. And that's when things get interesting. In the Bolivian Amazon, LiDAR revealed an extensive network of settlements associated with the CASARABE CULTURE, which flourished roughly between AD 500 and 1400. Researchers found large settlements, monumental earthen structures, raised causeways, canals and reservoirs. Some of the monumental structures rise more than twenty meters above the surrounding landscape. [pause] Look at the forest from the ground and you might see nothing unusual. Look at the same place through the LiDAR data and the landscape suddenly makes sense. Roads connect settlements. Water was being managed. Huge structures had been deliberately constructed. This wasn't simply a few people passing through a forest. It was a human landscape. And that discovery matters because for a long time, the Amazon was described as if dense rainforest and large, organized societies couldn't exist together. The archaeological evidence tells us the situation was much more complicated. There is even a historical clue that was recorded centuries before modern LiDAR. In 1541 and 1542, the expedition led by FRANCISCO DE ORELLANA traveled down the Amazon, and Friar Gaspar de Carvajal recorded what the expedition encountered. His account described substantial settlements and populations along parts of the river. For a long time, historians debated how much of those descriptions should be trusted. Some of the expedition's claims were extraordinary, and later Europeans encountered an Amazon that looked very different. Disease, violence and disruption had already transformed Indigenous populations in many areas. But archaeology is now providing independent evidence that parts of pre-Columbian Amazonia really did contain substantial settlements and engineered landscapes. That doesn't mean every detail in Carvajal's account was correct. It means the basic idea that large communities once existed along parts of the Amazon is no longer as difficult to accept. [pause] And that brings us to one of the most famous legends ever attached to South America: EL DORADO. For centuries, European explorers searched for a place of unimaginable wealth. Gold. Precious objects. A powerful ruler. Somewhere deep in the interior, they believed an extraordinary kingdom might be waiting. People died looking for it. But the original story was different. El Dorado wasn't originally the name of a golden city. It referred to a “gilded one,” associated with a Muisca ruler and a ceremony involving gold and Lake Guatavita. Somewhere along the way, a story about a person became a story about a kingdom. Then the kingdom became a city. And the farther the story traveled, the bigger the legend became. [soft] The golden city was never found. But there is an interesting twist. The explorers were searching for treasure in a region that really did contain complex societies and landscapes they barely understood. They were looking for gold while much of the actual history remained invisible. And the Amazon is extremely good at making human traces disappear. Give a road hundreds of years without maintenance and vegetation begins taking it back. Roots break through the ground. Soil accumulates. Trees grow across the old path. Eventually, something that was once carefully engineered can look completely natural. Now imagine that happening to an entire settlement. That's why LiDAR has been so important. It isn't just revealing individual ruins. In some places, it is revealing the shape of entire landscapes. [somber] There is another part of the Amazon story that requires even more care: the Indigenous peoples who still live there. Brazil's FUNAI recognizes confirmed groups living in voluntary isolation, along with other records under investigation. These communities aren't simply mysterious groups waiting to be discovered. Their isolation has a history. Previous contact with outsiders has brought disease, violence, displacement and devastating population loss to Indigenous communities. For people who have had little or no previous exposure to certain diseases, even an ordinary infection can become extremely dangerous. That's why Brazilian authorities have policies designed to protect isolated peoples from forced contact. Their presence is also a reminder that the Amazon isn't just a place with a hidden past. It is a place where people live, have lived for generations, and continue to make their own choices about contact with the outside world. And if you want to understand just how difficult the environment can be for an outsider, consider ED STAFFORD. In 2008, Stafford began an attempt to walk the length of the Amazon River. He completed the journey after 860 DAYS, becoming the first person recognized by Guinness World Records to walk the length of the river. He had to deal with heat, rain, mud, insects, rivers, exhaustion and the constant problem of simply moving forward through an enormous and difficult environment. [pause] You don't need a mythical monster to make the Amazon dangerous. The landscape itself is enough. But the forest also contains natural phenomena that sound like legends until you examine them closely. In Peru, there's a geothermal river known as SHANAY-TIMPISHKA, often called the Boiling River. Its waters can become dangerously hot because of geothermal activity. The surrounding rainforest looks ordinary, but the river itself can reach temperatures capable of causing severe burns. And then there are the stories connected to the rivers. Across Amazonian traditions, enormous serpents appear in stories associated with water and the natural world. One well-known example is YACUMAMA, often translated as “Mother of Water.” Yacumama belongs to mythology and tradition, not zoology, and there is no good scientific evidence for an enormous living serpent of the kind described in modern retellings. But there really was a giant snake in prehistoric South America. Its name was TITANOBOA. And this is where we need to be precise. Titanoboa did not live in the modern Amazon Basin. Its fossils were discovered in the Cerrejón Formation in what is now northern Colombia. Around 58 to 60 million years ago, that region was a hot tropical rainforest environment. Titanoboa may have reached roughly 42 to 49 feet in length, making it one of the largest snakes known to science. [pause] It wasn't a monster hiding in an Amazon river today. It was a prehistoric animal from a completely different world. But its existence tells us something fascinating about ancient tropical South America. The ecosystems of the distant past could support animals on a scale that seems almost impossible when compared with the world we know today. And the same problem of separating popular stories from scientific reality appears again when we talk about the Amazon itself. You've probably heard it called the LUNGS OF THE EARTH. You've probably also heard that the Amazon produces twenty percent of the world's oxygen. The phrase is memorable. The number is repeated everywhere. But the science is more complicated. The Amazon produces oxygen through photosynthesis, but plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms also consume oxygen and break down organic matter. Over long periods, much of the oxygen produced within the forest is balanced by respiration and decomposition. Meanwhile, NOAA estimates that roughly half of Earth's oxygen production comes from the ocean, with microscopic photosynthetic organisms such as phytoplankton playing a major role. So the Amazon isn't a giant oxygen machine producing one-fifth of the air humanity breathes. [pause] And correcting that myth doesn't make the forest less important. It points us toward what actually makes it important. The Amazon is a huge part of the global carbon cycle. Its vegetation stores carbon and moves enormous amounts of water between the ground and atmosphere. That water cycle influences rainfall, while the forest supports extraordinary biodiversity and millions of people with deep cultural and historical relationships to these landscapes. So the real story isn't that the Amazon is keeping the planet alive by manufacturing oxygen. It's that the forest is part of a much larger system involving carbon, water, climate and life. And that system is changing. When forests are cleared or burned, carbon stored in vegetation and soils can enter the atmosphere. Water cycles can be disrupted. Habitats disappear. And once an ecosystem has been heavily altered, simply planting trees doesn't instantly recreate everything that existed before. The legendary giant snake belongs to mythology, while a real prehistoric giant snake lived millions of years ago in what is now Colombia. The famous oxygen claim turns out to be far more complicated than the slogan. Even the people living in the forest have histories and perspectives that outsiders have repeatedly misunderstood. [pause] That's what makes the Amazon so fascinating. Not every legend is true. Not every monster exists. But the truth underneath some of these stories can still be extraordinary.
